Annual rate contract procurement: a practical guide

How to plan, negotiate, approve, roll out and renew an annual rate contract, so the rates you agree are the rates your buyers actually pay.

Published 12 November 2026·15 min read·By ProcEaze

An annual rate contract (ARC) is one of the most widely used tools in Indian industrial and indirect procurement, and one of the most frequently under-used. Negotiating good rates is the visible part of the work. Making sure those rates are used, by every buyer at every site, for the whole year, is where most of the value is won or lost.

This guide walks through the full ARC cycle: deciding what belongs on a rate contract, preparing the item list, running the RFQ, agreeing commercial terms, getting approval, rolling the contract out, monitoring compliance and renewing it. It describes common practice; contract wording with legal or tax consequences should always be reviewed by your own legal and finance teams.

What is an annual rate contract?

An annual rate contract is an agreement with a vendor that fixes the price, and the main commercial terms, for a defined list of items over a period, usually twelve months. It is often simply called a rate contract. Unlike a normal purchase order, an ARC does not usually commit you to buy a fixed quantity. It commits the vendor to supply at the agreed rate whenever you order, within the terms of the contract.

In practice, an ARC sits between sourcing and buying. Procurement negotiates once, against a year's expected demand. Buyers and plants then raise purchase requisitions and purchase orders against the contract as and when they need the items, without going back to the market each time. Each such order is sometimes called a call-off.

Most organisations use ARCs for repeat, predictable items: safety and PPE, MRO spares, electricals, fasteners, bearings, lubricants, office supplies and IT consumables. These are the categories where the same items are bought again and again by many people across sites, and where negotiating every order separately costs more effort than it saves.

A typical ARC records:

  • the list of items, with specifications, make or approved equivalents, and unit of measure
  • the agreed rate per unit and what that rate includes (the price basis)
  • any price-variation clause
  • the MOQ, delivery lead time and delivery locations
  • payment terms
  • the validity period, and the conditions for renewal or termination
  • an indicative annual quantity, where one is shared

Every abbreviation used in this guide is explained in the procurement glossary.

When to use an ARC, and when to buy spot

An ARC is not the right tool for every purchase. It works best when four conditions hold:

  • Repeat demand. The item is bought several times a year, not once.
  • A stable specification. Everyone means the same item when they order it, so a single rate makes sense.
  • Many buyers or sites. Orders spread across people and locations are where prices drift most without a contract.
  • A reasonably predictable volume. You can give vendors an honest indicative quantity, which is what earns a better rate.

Spot buying, where you raise an RFQ or buy against a fresh quotation for each requirement, usually suits one-off items, project-specific materials, items whose specification changes from order to order, and commodities whose prices move so fast that no vendor will hold a rate for long without adding a large risk margin.

Most organisations end up with a mix: ARCs for the high-frequency core of their catalogue, and competitive spot buying for everything else. The comparison rate contract vs spot buying sets out the trade-offs side by side.

A simple test is to list last year's purchase orders by item. Items that were ordered many times, by more than one person, at prices that varied between orders, are your strongest ARC candidates.

Preparing the item list and specifications

The item list is the foundation of the contract. If it is vague, vendors will price the vagueness in, buyers will find that items don't quite match what they need, and purchases will drift outside the contract.

Start from your own purchase history

Pull twelve to twenty-four months of PO lines for the category. Group them by item, clean up duplicate descriptions (the same pair of gloves described five different ways is common), and note the quantity, number of orders, sites and the range of prices paid. This gives you an indicative annual quantity and shows where prices have varied most. If your item master has many duplicates, cleaning it up now will also make the contract easier to enforce later.

Write specifications vendors can price

For each line, give the description, the technical specification or standard (such as the relevant IS or EN standard), the make or list of approved makes, whether equivalents are acceptable, the pack size, the unit of measure and the HSN code. Ambiguity in any of these is a common source of disputes later in the year.

Decide on brand or equivalent

Locking a single brand gives users certainty but limits competition. Allowing approved equivalents widens the field, but someone, usually the user department or quality team, needs to approve which equivalents are acceptable before the RFQ, not after it.

Group items into lots

Grouping related items into lots, such as all hand protection or all LT cables, lets specialist vendors quote for what they do well. Decide in advance whether you will award by line, by lot or for the whole list, and say so in the RFQ.

Vendor selection and the RFQ for an ARC

An ARC RFQ is like any request for quotation, with one important difference: you are asking for a rate that will hold for a year, across sites, against an indicative rather than committed quantity. Vendors need enough information to price that risk properly.

Build the vendor list

Invite vendors who can actually service the contract: the right categories, coverage of your delivery locations, the capacity for your volumes, and a valid GSTIN. For larger contracts, many organisations run a short supplier evaluation covering quality, delivery record, financial standing and compliance documents before inviting a vendor to quote. The supplier evaluation template gives you a weighted scoring structure to start from.

What the RFQ should contain

  • the item list with specifications and indicative annual quantities by location
  • the contract period and expected start date
  • the price basis you want quoted
  • your proposed terms on price variation, MOQ, lead time and payment
  • a fixed response format, so quotes can be compared line by line
  • the closing date and how clarifications will be handled
  • the evaluation method: lowest price, or a techno-commercial evaluation that also weighs quality and service

The RFQ template generator produces a structured RFQ you can adapt for an ARC.

Evaluate and negotiate

Put the quotes into a comparative statement, normalising for price basis, freight, taxes and payment terms so that you compare like with like. Identify the lowest quote, the L1, by line or by lot. Negotiation usually follows, through direct discussion or a reverse auction, and the final rates should be recorded alongside the original quotes.

Some organisations also award a second vendor a smaller share as a backup source, at or near the L1 rate. If you plan to do this, state it in the RFQ so vendors can price accordingly.

Commercial terms: price basis, price variation, MOQ, validity, delivery and payment

The terms around the rate matter as much as the rate itself. A good price with an impractical MOQ, a vague price basis or an unworkable lead time will not hold up in use. The summary below reflects common practice; your own terms will depend on the category, your sites and your policies.

Price basis

The price basis says what the rate includes. Two common options are ex-works, where you arrange and pay for transport from the vendor's premises, and FOR destination, where the vendor delivers to your site and the rate includes freight. Spell out whether packing, loading, unloading and insurance are included, and whether GST is included or extra. Most B2B contracts quote rates exclusive of GST, with the applicable rate shown separately.

Price-variation clauses

For items linked to volatile raw materials, such as copper or aluminium cables, steel products and some chemicals, vendors may not be willing to hold a fixed rate for twelve months. A price-variation clause links the rate to a published index or base price through an agreed formula.

The usual design questions are: which index, what base date and base value, what share of the price is variable, whether there is a band within which no change is made, how often rates are revised, and whether a revision applies to orders already placed. For everything else, a firm rate for the contract period is simpler to administer and far easier to monitor.

MOQ and pack sizes

A minimum order quantity protects the vendor from uneconomic small orders. Agree MOQs per item or per order value, and make sure buyers can see them when they order. An MOQ that is out of line with how a site actually consumes an item will push buyers towards local purchases.

Validity

State the start and end dates, and what happens at the end: whether the contract can be extended at the same rates for a short period while a renewal is negotiated, and with how much notice. Avoid open-ended validity.

Delivery terms

Agree lead times by item or category, the delivery locations, and the documents that must travel with the goods: the invoice, test certificates where required and the e-way bill where applicable. Say how short or late deliveries will be handled. If you have many sites, specify whether lead times differ by location.

Payment terms

Payment terms are usually expressed as a number of days from receipt of goods, GRN or invoice. Keep them consistent with your accounts payable process, and remember that payments to registered micro and small suppliers are subject to statutory time limits (see MSME). Your finance team will know how these apply to you.

Other clauses to consider

  • the quality inspection and rejection process
  • warranty, for items where it applies
  • periodic performance review, and the right to terminate for poor service
  • how rates change if statutory tax rates change
  • confidentiality of rates and volumes

Approvals and the NFA

Before an ARC is awarded, the decision normally needs approval under your Delegation of Authority (DoA). Because an ARC covers a year of spend, the approval level is usually set by the estimated annual contract value, not the value of a single order.

The approval document is commonly called a Note for Approval (NFA). A good NFA lets an approver understand and approve the decision without opening another file. It typically includes:

  • the requirement: category, items, sites and contract period
  • the estimated annual value, and how it was estimated
  • the vendors invited, those who responded, and any excluded, with reasons
  • the comparative statement, with the L1 position by line or lot
  • the negotiation history and the final rates
  • a comparison with last purchase prices or the previous contract
  • the key commercial terms, including any price-variation clause
  • the recommendation and the reasons for it, especially where it is not L1 on every line

Record each approval with the approver's name and the date. When the contract is audited, the NFA and its audit trail are the first documents anyone will ask for. The article on designing a procurement approval matrix covers how to set approval levels that protect the organisation without slowing routine buying down.

Rolling the ARC out to buyers

Signing the ARC is only half the job. Many contracts under-deliver because the agreed rates never reach the people who place orders. A practical rollout covers five things:

  1. Publish the rates where buyers order. A PDF on a shared drive is easy to miss. The rates, MOQs and validity belong in the catalogue, ordering screen or item master that buyers actually use.
  2. Map items to item codes. If your ERP has several codes for the same item, link all of them to the contract, or clean them up.
  3. Brief the users. Tell buyers, stores and user departments what is covered, who the vendor is, the lead times and the process for anything that isn't covered.
  4. Set the exception route. Decide what a buyer should do when an item is needed urgently and the ARC vendor cannot deliver in time, and who approves that exception.
  5. Share contacts and escalation. Give sites the vendor contacts and a clear path for delivery or quality problems.

Catalogue buying, where contracted items and rates are the default choice on the ordering screen, is the most reliable way to make the contract the path of least resistance.

Monitoring compliance and leakage

Leakage is the gap between the rates you negotiated and the prices you actually paid. It appears in a few predictable ways: contracted items bought from other vendors, contracted items bought at a higher rate, and items ordered under a slightly different description so they never match the contract at all. Spend that bypasses agreed contracts is often called maverick spend.

Track a small number of measures every month:

  • ARC utilisation: the share of spend on contracted items that went through the contract
  • Price compliance: PO lines on contracted items where the price differs from the ARC rate
  • Off-contract purchases of contracted items, by site and by buyer
  • Vendor performance: on-time delivery, short supply and rejections
  • Consumption against the indicative quantity, so you know early if volumes are running well above or below plan

The point of monitoring is to find causes, not to blame buyers. If one site keeps buying locally, the reason is often a lead time, MOQ or service problem with the ARC vendor, and that is usually fixable. The post why negotiated rates don’t reach the purchase order looks at the causes and fixes in more detail.

Renewals

Start the renewal well before the contract expires. For most categories, two to three months ahead is a sensible minimum; allow longer for large or complex contracts, so there is time to go back to the market if needed. A renewal review should cover:

  • actual consumption against the indicative quantity
  • items to add, remove or re-specify, based on what was bought off-contract during the year
  • the vendor's delivery and quality performance
  • price movement in the market, and whether any price-variation clause worked as intended
  • whether to extend, renegotiate with the incumbent or re-tender

If the renewal will not be ready in time, agree a short, documented extension at the existing rates rather than letting the contract lapse. A lapsed contract leaves buyers ordering without any agreed rate, and the leakage that follows can be hard to reverse.

Common mistakes

  • Vague specifications. Vendors price the uncertainty in, and disputes follow.
  • Inflated indicative quantities. Overstating volume to win a lower rate damages trust when the volume doesn't arrive, and vendors remember it at the next tender.
  • No stated price basis. Rates that don't say what they include cannot be compared, and freight or packing appears later as an extra.
  • Contracts nobody can find. Rates that aren't visible at the point of order are, in practice, optional.
  • No exception route. If there is no quick, approved way to handle urgent needs, buyers will create their own.
  • Ignoring MOQs and lead times. A good rate with an impractical MOQ or lead time drives local buying.
  • Letting contracts lapse. Expired rates quietly turn back into spot buying.
  • Not measuring. Without utilisation and price-compliance figures, nobody knows whether the contract is working.

ARC checklist

  • Purchase history analysed and ARC candidate items identified
  • Item list cleaned, with specifications, makes, units and HSN codes
  • Indicative annual quantities by site
  • Vendor list prepared and vendors evaluated
  • RFQ issued with a fixed response format, price basis and evaluation method
  • Comparative statement prepared on a like-for-like basis
  • Negotiation completed and final rates recorded
  • Price basis, price variation, MOQ, validity, delivery and payment terms agreed
  • NFA approved under your Delegation of Authority
  • Contract signed and rates published where buyers order
  • Buyers, stores and user departments briefed; exception route agreed
  • Monthly utilisation, price-compliance and vendor performance reporting in place
  • Renewal review scheduled well before expiry
