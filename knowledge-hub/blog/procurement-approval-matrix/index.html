How to design a procurement approval matrix (DoA) that doesn't slow buying down

A procurement approval matrix should protect the organisation without anyone noticing it is there. Here is how to design one that does.

Every organisation needs a Delegation of Authority (DoA) for purchasing: a clear statement of who can approve what, and up to which value. The approval matrix is how that delegation is applied to each purchase request.

Designed well, an approval matrix protects the organisation from unauthorised or poorly justified spending without anyone noticing it is there. Designed badly, it becomes the reason a ₹2,000 spare takes two weeks to order, and buyers start finding ways around it. This article sets out how to design one that does the first and avoids the second.

What an approval matrix does

An approval matrix answers three questions for every purchase request: does this need approval, who must approve it, and in what order? The answer usually depends on a few factors:

  • the value of the request
  • whether the item is on an existing contract or not
  • the category, for example capital items, IT or hazardous materials
  • the department, cost centre or site raising it
  • whether the purchase is budgeted

The DoA is the policy; the matrix is the operating rule. Keeping the two aligned, and keeping the matrix simple enough to apply consistently, is most of the design work.

Why approval matrices slow buying down

Slow approvals are rarely caused by approvers being slow. They are usually built into the design:

  • Too many levels for low-value requests. A routine consumable passes through the same chain as a large order.
  • Approving transactions that were already approved. Each call-off against an approved rate contract goes through a full approval again, even though the vendor and price were approved when the contract was awarded.
  • Serial chains. Every approver waits for the one before, so one absence holds up everything.
  • No delegates. When an approver is travelling or on leave, requests simply wait.
  • Unclear rules. If buyers can't predict who will approve a request, they can't plan for it.

Design principles

Approve decisions, not transactions

The most effective change in many organisations is to separate the sourcing decision from the order. When a rate contract is awarded, the vendor, items and rates are approved once, through the NFA. Orders placed against that contract, at the contracted rate, then need a lighter approval: usually budget and need, not price and vendor. Off-contract purchases carry the full chain, because the price and vendor have not been approved yet.

Use a small number of value bands

Three to five value bands are usually enough. Each band should reflect a real step up in risk, and the thresholds should be reviewed periodically, not left unchanged for years while prices rise.

Keep levels proportionate

Low-value requests should need one approver, typically the requester's manager or department head. Additional levels should be added only where they genuinely add judgement: finance for budget, a plant head for site impact, senior management for large or unusual commitments.

Set delegates and time limits

Every approver should have a named delegate, and every level an expected turnaround time. Reminders and escalation after that time keep requests moving without anyone chasing.

Keep segregation of duties

Whatever the design, segregation of duties still applies: the person requesting should not be the person approving, and approval should be separate from ordering and receiving.

Record everything

Approvals should be recorded with the approver's name, the action, the date and any comments, in a permanent audit trail. That record is what makes a lighter process acceptable to auditors.

An illustrative structure

The table below shows the shape of a simple matrix. The value bands are examples only, not a recommendation. Set your own bands and approvers to match your DoA, your organisation's size and your risk appetite.

Illustrative approval matrix structure
Value band (example only)On-contract (ARC rate)Off-contract
Up to ₹1 lakhDepartment headDepartment head, then procurement
₹1 lakh to ₹10 lakhDepartment headDepartment head, procurement, then plant or function head
₹10 lakh to ₹50 lakhDepartment head, then financeDepartment head, procurement, finance, then plant or function head
Above ₹50 lakhDepartment head, finance, then senior managementFull chain to senior management, with NFA

Notice the pattern: the on-contract route stays short at every value, because the commercial decision was approved when the contract was awarded. The off-contract route grows with value, because each request is a new commercial decision.

Common mistakes

  • Copying another organisation's matrix. Thresholds and approvers should reflect your structure, not someone else's.
  • Splitting orders to stay under a threshold. If this happens often, the threshold is probably too low, and the matrix should also look at cumulative spend, not just single requests.
  • Adding a level after every incident. Each addition feels safe, but together they produce a chain nobody can move through.
  • Leaving the matrix on paper. If routing depends on people remembering the rules, they will be applied inconsistently.

Review it once a year

Look at approval data once a year: average time per level, requests sent back or rejected, and how often each level actually changes an outcome. A level that approves everything it sees, quickly, may be adding little control. A level where requests wait longest may need a delegate or a lower threshold. Then update the DoA and the matrix together.
