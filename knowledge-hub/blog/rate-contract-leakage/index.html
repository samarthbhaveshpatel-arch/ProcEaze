Why negotiated rates don't reach the purchase order, and how to fix it

Rate contract leakage is rarely about bad intent. It is usually about where rates live, how items are coded and what buyers do when the contract doesn't fit.

Published 12 November 2026·4 min read·By ProcEaze

Most procurement teams have been through this. A category is tendered, vendors are evaluated, rates are negotiated and approved, and an annual rate contract is signed. Six months later, someone looks at the purchase orders and finds the same items bought from other vendors, at other prices, at several sites.

This gap between the contract and the purchase order is usually called spend leakage. It is rarely the result of bad intent. It is almost always the result of how the contract was rolled out and how buying actually happens. That is good news, because it means it can be fixed.

What leakage looks like

Leakage tends to show up in four forms:

  • Wrong vendor. A contracted item is bought from a vendor who is not on the contract, often a local supplier.
  • Wrong price. The right vendor is used, but the PO carries a different rate from the contract, usually an older or higher one.
  • Wrong item. A near-identical item is ordered under a different description or item code, so it never matches the contract.
  • No contract. The contract has expired and nobody noticed, so orders continue at whatever price is quoted.

Each of these is a form of maverick spend, and each has a different cause. Treating them all as buyer discipline problems is the most common reason leakage persists.

Why it happens

The rates are not visible where orders are placed

If the contract lives in a PDF, an email or a spreadsheet on the procurement team's drive, a buyer at a plant has to know it exists, find it and look up the item before ordering. Under time pressure, many won't. The contract may be excellent, but in practice it is optional.

The item master doesn't match the contract

When the same item has several codes or descriptions in the item master, buyers can order a perfectly valid code that simply isn't linked to the contract. Nothing flags it, and the price is whatever the vendor quotes.

MOQ, lead time or service gaps

Sometimes buyers go elsewhere for a good reason. The contracted vendor's MOQ may be larger than a site needs, the lead time may be too long for a breakdown, or deliveries to a remote site may be unreliable. Each off-contract order is then a rational local decision, and the contract is the real problem.

Urgency without an exception route

Breakdowns happen. If there is no quick, approved way to handle an urgent need, buyers create their own, and the workaround becomes the habit.

Contracts that lapse

When a renewal is late, the old rates stop applying and nothing replaces them. Orders placed in the gap are effectively spot purchases, often at higher prices, and they can continue long after the new contract is signed.

Nobody is measuring

If no one reports on contract utilisation and price compliance, leakage is invisible until an audit or a cost review finds it.

How to fix it

Fixing leakage is mostly about making the contracted route the easiest route. In rough order of impact:

  1. Put contracted rates at the point of order. Load the items, rates, MOQs and validity into the catalogue, ordering screen or item master buyers actually use, so the contracted item is the default choice.
  2. Lock the price. Where a contract exists, the buyer shouldn't be able to edit the rate on the order. A price that can't be changed can't drift.
  3. Clean up item codes. Merge duplicate codes, or link every equivalent code to the contract line.
  4. Fix the contract, not just the behaviour. Where a site keeps buying locally, find out why. An MOQ, a lead time or a delivery location may need renegotiating.
  5. Create a fast exception route. Allow urgent off-contract purchases with a short, defined approval, and record the reason so you can see patterns.
  6. Track renewals. Keep a renewal calendar and start reviews two to three months before expiry, so contracts never lapse unnoticed.
  7. Report monthly. Share utilisation and price-compliance figures by site and category with the people who can act on them.

The annual rate contract procurement guide covers how to build these steps into the contract from the start, rather than fixing them afterwards.

What to measure

You don't need a large set of measures. Four, tracked consistently, will tell you whether contracts are working:

  • ARC utilisation: of all spend on contracted items, the share that went through the contract
  • Price compliance: the number and value of PO lines on contracted items where the price differs from the contract rate
  • Off-contract purchases by reason: urgency, MOQ, stock-out or no reason given
  • Contracts expiring in the next 90 days, with renewal status

Read these by site and by category, not just in total. Leakage is usually concentrated in a few places, and that is where the fixes will pay off first.
