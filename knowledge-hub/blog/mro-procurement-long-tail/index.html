MRO procurement: why the long tail costs more than it looks

Small MRO orders look harmless one at a time. Together, the long tail carries process cost, price variation, vendor sprawl and downtime risk.

Published 12 November 2026·5 min read·By ProcEaze

MRO (maintenance, repair and operations) procurement covers the spares, consumables, tools and supplies that keep plants, warehouses and offices running: bearings, fasteners, electrical fittings, PPE, lubricants, hand tools, cleaning supplies. None of it goes into the product. Much of it is low in value per order. And because of that, it is easy to treat as unimportant.

The problem is the shape of MRO spend. A relatively small number of items and vendors account for most of the value, and then there is a long tail: thousands of items, many vendors, small orders, bought by many people. This tail spend usually costs far more to manage than its value suggests.

What the long tail looks like

If you list a year's MRO purchase lines by item and sort them by value, the pattern is familiar. The top of the list is a manageable set of high-value or high-frequency items. Below it runs a long list of items bought once or twice, in small quantities, from a wide spread of vendors, often local ones.

Typical features of the tail:

  • many vendors, each supplying a handful of items
  • the same item bought from different vendors, at different prices, by different sites
  • inconsistent item descriptions and codes in the item master
  • urgent orders raised when something breaks down
  • purchases made through petty cash or reimbursement, outside the procurement system

Why it costs more than it looks

Process cost per order

Every purchase order carries a roughly similar process: a requisition, quotes, a comparison, approvals, a PO, receipt, an invoice and a payment. That effort is much the same for a ₹3,000 order as for a ₹3 lakh one. Across thousands of small orders, the people-time spent on the process can be large compared with the value of what is bought.

Price variation

Without a contract, the same item is priced afresh each time. Different sites pay different prices for the same bearing or glove, and nobody sees the spread because no one report shows it.

Vendor sprawl

Each additional vendor needs onboarding, a vendor master record, GST and bank checks, and payment processing. A long list of occasional vendors multiplies that work and the compliance exposure that comes with it.

Duplicate stock and items

When the same item exists under several codes, stores may hold it in several places, or order it while it is already on the shelf under another description.

Downtime risk

The real cost of an MRO item is often not its price but the cost of not having it. A missing spare can stop a line. When tail items have no agreed source or lead time, every breakdown becomes an emergency purchase.

A weak audit trail

Small purchases are the most likely to bypass the system altogether, through cash purchases or informal orders. That leaves gaps in records that auditors and finance teams have to reconstruct later.

Signs your MRO tail needs attention

You don't need a full analysis to know whether the tail is a problem. A few signs usually show up first:

  • buyers spend more of their week chasing quotes for small items than managing contracts or vendors
  • the vendor master grows every quarter, and many vendors receive only one or two orders a year
  • the same item appears at noticeably different prices on purchase orders from different sites
  • stores report items on the shelf under one code while another code for the same item is being ordered
  • emergency purchases after breakdowns are routine rather than rare
  • finance regularly asks for supporting documents on small purchases that were made outside the system

If several of these sound familiar, the long tail is probably costing more in time, price and risk than its value suggests.

Why the usual fixes fall short

The common responses each solve part of the problem. Tightening approvals adds control but also adds process cost to every small order, which makes the original problem worse. Cutting the vendor list without giving buyers an alternative pushes purchases into cash and reimbursements. Running a large tender for the whole tail takes a long time and often struggles with poor item data.

What works better is to reduce the number of separate buying decisions, not just to add checks to each of them.

How to bring the tail under control

  1. Analyse the spend. Run a spend analysis of twelve to twenty-four months of MRO purchases by item, vendor, site and frequency. Clean up descriptions as you go.
  2. Split the tail. Separate items that recur (bought several times, by several sites) from genuine one-offs.
  3. Contract the recurring items. Put recurring items on annual rate contracts, grouped into category lots, with clear specifications, MOQs and lead times.
  4. Make the catalogue the default. Publish contracted items and rates where buyers order, so catalogue buying is quicker than raising a fresh RFQ.
  5. Consolidate vendors. Fewer vendors with broader category coverage reduce onboarding and payment work, and make each relationship more valuable to both sides.
  6. Give one-offs a light, fast route. Use a simple RFQ process and a proportionate approval route for genuine one-off items.
  7. Measure and repeat. Track how much MRO spend goes through contracts, and move newly recurring items onto contracts at each renewal.

The MRO procurement checklist sets out what to check before you buy, and rate contract vs spot buying helps decide which items belong on a contract.
