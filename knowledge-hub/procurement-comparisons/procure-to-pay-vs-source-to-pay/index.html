Procure-to-pay vs source-to-pay: the difference explained

P2P makes sure each purchase follows the decision. S2P also makes the decision. Here is where one ends and the other begins.

Procure-to-pay (P2P) and source-to-pay (S2P) are often used as if they meant the same thing. They don't. P2P covers the buying and paying of things you have already decided to buy, from a known source. S2P includes P2P, and adds everything that happens before it: understanding spend, sourcing, choosing vendors and agreeing contracts.

The difference matters when you are deciding what to fix first, or what kind of software you need. This comparison sets out where each process starts and ends, who is involved and what each one controls.

The two processes

Procure-to-pay

P2P begins when someone needs something. A purchase requisition is raised and approved, a purchase order is issued, goods are received and recorded on a GRN, the vendor's invoice is checked through a three-way match, and the vendor is paid. P2P assumes the vendor and price are already known, either from a contract or from a quote approved for this purchase.

Source-to-pay

S2P starts earlier. It begins with spend analysis to understand what is bought and from whom, moves through sourcing, RFQs or reverse auctions, vendor selection and contracting, and includes ongoing supplier management. Only then does it hand over to P2P for day-to-day buying and payment.

How they fit together

The handover point between the two is the contract, or the approved sourcing decision. Sourcing decides which vendor supplies which item at what price and on what terms; P2P makes sure each purchase follows that decision.

That is why weaknesses in one show up in the other. A strong sourcing process that agrees good rates delivers little if P2P lets buyers order at other prices. A tight P2P process can only enforce the rates and vendors it has been given; if sourcing never revisits them, P2P efficiently pays prices that may no longer be competitive.

A worked example

Take a plant that needs safety gloves every month.

On the source-to-pay side, procurement reviews last year's glove purchases across sites, writes a specification, invites qualified vendors to quote, compares the responses, negotiates, and gets an annual rate contract approved through an NFA. The contract records the item, rate, MOQ, lead time and payment terms. Through the year, procurement reviews the vendor's delivery and quality, and decides at renewal whether to extend or re-tender.

On the procure-to-pay side, a stores officer raises a requisition for gloves each month. It is approved under the approval matrix, a purchase order is issued at the contracted rate, the delivery is recorded on a GRN, the invoice is matched against the PO and GRN, and the vendor is paid.

The sourcing work happens once a year. The P2P cycle runs twelve times. If the P2P side doesn't enforce the contract, the sourcing work is wasted; if the sourcing side is never revisited, P2P keeps paying last year's price.

Common misconceptions

  • “P2P is just accounts payable.” Payment is the last step, but most of the control in P2P sits earlier: in requisitions, approvals, price compliance and goods receipt.
  • “S2P replaces P2P.” It contains it. An organisation with strong sourcing still needs a reliable P2P process to turn sourcing decisions into correct orders and payments.
  • “You must do everything at once.” Most organisations build in stages, and the order depends on where the biggest gaps are today.

Which to fix first

There is no universal answer, but a few signs point one way or the other:

  • Start with P2P if you already have contracts but can't tell whether they are used, approvals are slow or inconsistent, purchase orders are re-keyed by hand, or audit questions take days to answer.
  • Start with sourcing if most spend is not under any contract, vendors have not been re-tendered in years, or you have little idea how prices compare across sites.
  • Do both in stages if you have many sites and categories. A common sequence is to put contracted, catalogue buying in place first, then extend into sourcing. The ARC procurement guide shows how the two meet in a rate contract.
