Rate contract vs spot buying: which to use, and when

Neither route is better in general. The real question is which items belong on a rate contract and which are better bought spot.

Published 12 November 2026·5 min read·By ProcEaze

Most organisations buy in two ways. Some items are bought under a rate contract, usually an annual rate contract (ARC), where rates are agreed in advance and orders are placed against them through the year. Everything else is bought through spot buying: quotes are requested for each requirement as it arises.

Neither is better in general. Each suits different kinds of items, and the real question is which items belong in which route. This comparison sets out the trade-offs and offers a simple way to decide.

The two approaches

Rate contract

Procurement runs one sourcing exercise for a list of items, negotiates rates and terms, and gets the award approved, usually through an NFA. Buyers then order against the contract as needed, at the agreed rates, without returning to the market. The contract carries an indicative quantity but usually no firm commitment to buy it.

Spot buying

Each requirement is sourced when it arises. The buyer raises an RFQ or requests quotes, compares them, gets approval and issues a purchase order. There is no standing agreement, so each purchase is a fresh commercial decision.

When a rate contract wins

Rate contracts are strongest where the same items are bought repeatedly, by many people, across sites. In that situation, spot buying repeats the same commercial work again and again, and produces different prices for the same item. An ARC does that work once, gives vendors a reason to offer a better rate in return for a year of business, and makes ordering faster.

The catch is that a rate contract only delivers if buyers use it. Rates need to be visible and, ideally, locked at the point of order. Otherwise, contracted items drift back into spot buying at other prices, which is the problem described in why negotiated rates don’t reach the purchase order.

When spot buying wins

Spot buying is the better route for genuine one-off purchases, project-specific materials, items whose specification changes from order to order, and items whose prices move quickly. For volatile items, a vendor asked to hold a rate for a year will usually add a margin to cover the risk, so a well-run spot purchase can be the cheaper option.

It is also the right route for new requirements that you don't yet understand well. A few spot purchases can establish the specification, the vendor field and the price range before you decide whether a contract makes sense.

The usual answer: both

In practice, most organisations need both. A common pattern is a core of ARCs covering high-frequency items in categories such as PPE, MRO spares, electricals, fasteners and office supplies, with spot buying for everything else. Items that turn out to recur are moved onto contracts at the next renewal. For volatile but recurring items, a rate contract with a price-variation clause can combine the two: an agreed vendor and terms, with a rate that follows an index.

Common mistakes with each route

With rate contracts

  • putting items on contract that are bought once a year, so the contract adds administration without saving effort
  • overstating the indicative quantity to win a lower rate, then disappointing the vendor
  • loading rates where buyers can't see them, so orders drift back to spot buying
  • letting contracts lapse without a documented extension

With spot buying

  • buying the same item spot, again and again, long after it should have moved onto a contract
  • collecting too few quotes, or always from the same vendors, so the price is never really tested
  • comparing quotes on different price bases, for example one including freight and one excluding it
  • skipping the record of why a vendor was chosen, which leaves gaps at audit

Both routes depend on clean item data and a clear approval matrix: contracted orders can follow a short approval route, while spot purchases carry the full one.

A simple test

For each item or item group, ask:

  1. Was it ordered several times last year, by more than one person or site?
  2. Is the specification stable enough that everyone means the same item?
  3. Can you give vendors an honest indicative annual quantity?
  4. Is the price stable enough for a vendor to hold it, or to follow a simple index?

If the answer to all four is yes, it is a strong ARC candidate. The annual rate contract procurement guide covers how to set one up, and ARC contract pricing on ProcEaze shows how contracted rates are loaded and locked at the point of order, with MOQ and validity shown to buyers.
