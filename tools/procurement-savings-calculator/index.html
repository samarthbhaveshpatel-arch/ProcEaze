How it is calculated

Spend moved onto contract = annual spend × (target share − current share)
₹5,00,00,000 × (70% − 40%) = ₹1,50,00,000
Price savings = spend moved onto contract × price difference
₹1,50,00,000 × 5% = ₹7,50,000
Process savings = POs per year × process cost per PO × expected reduction
2,000 POs × ₹1,500 × 30% = ₹9,00,000
Total = price savings + process savings
₹7,50,000 + ₹9,00,000 = ₹16,50,000

This is an estimate, not a forecast. It is calculated only from the numbers and assumptions you enter. Actual results depend on your items, suppliers, prices and processes.

The formula

The calculator estimates two separate effects and adds them together.

  • Price savings = annual spend × (target share on contract − current share on contract) × price difference. The price difference is how much lower contract prices are, as a percentage of the off-contract price.
  • Process savings = purchase orders per year × process cost per PO × expected reduction in process cost.
  • Total = price savings + process savings.

If the target share is lower than the current share, price savings are taken as zero. Percentages above 100 are capped at 100.

Getting realistic inputs

Spend and contract share: take the last 12 months of indirect and MRO purchases from your ERP or purchase register, and the part bought at contracted rates.

Price difference: compare off-contract purchase prices with the contract rate for the same items, and use a spend-weighted average rather than the best example.

Process cost per PO: estimate the time requesters, buyers, approvers and accounts spend per order, multiplied by their cost per hour. Be conservative about the reduction you expect.

Tips

Using the estimate well.

  • Run it three times: cautious, expected and optimistic assumptions. Present the range, not one number.
  • Only count spend that can realistically go on contract. One-off and specialised items often cannot.
  • Process savings are usually time freed up, not cash, unless headcount or overtime actually changes.
  • Write your assumptions next to the result when you share it, so others can challenge them.
